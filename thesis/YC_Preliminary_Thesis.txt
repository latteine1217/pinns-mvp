Chapter 1  Introduction 

1.1  Background. 

1.1.1  Liquid rocket engine 

Liquid rocket engines (LREs) used to be missiles, aircraft and Jet assisted system [1]. Currently they 

are all concerned with space flight and have served as the primary propulsion systems for most launch 

vehicles and spacecraft for decades. Their advantage lies in the ability to be designed across a wide 

range of thrust levels to suit specific launch stages and  spacecraft requirements. This flexibility is 

enabled  by  liquid  bipropellants,  which  provide  higher  specific  impulse  than  other  chemical 

propulsion systems. In LREs, the injector atomizes the liquid propellants (fuel and oxidizer), which 

10 

mix and react in  the  chamber and  combustion generates high-temperature, high-pressure products 

11 

which  exhausts  are  accelerated  to  supersonic  speeds  through  the  nozzle  to  make  thrust  based  on 

12 

Newton’s  third  law  of  motion  (see  Figure  1.1).  Improving  the  performance  of  future  LREs  may 

13 

require  increase  in  internal  pressure  and  heat  flux  that  already  substantially.  There  are  many 

14 

challenges  to  engine  life,  reliability,  safety  and  pollutants  emission.  To  address  this  issue,  it  is 

15 

essential to fully understand the chemical and physical processes involved, and to develop suitable 

16 

models that can be integrated into advanced analytical tools.

1.1.2  Rocket Propellant 1 (RP-1) surrogate 

23 

Rocket  Propellant  1  (RP-1)  is  a  well-established  hydrocarbon  fuel  that  has  been  widely  used  in 

24 

propulsion systems. Typically paired with oxygen as a bipropellant since its development in the 1950s 

25 

[2]. RP-1 is derived from a specific kerosene fraction, which undergoes additional refining processes 

26 

to remove unsaturated compounds and sulfur-containing hydrocarbons by acid washing and sulfur 

27 

dioxide extraction [3]. Generally, distillates with a high paraffinic content from crude oil are selected 

28 

for RP-1 production to ensure that the fuel meets critical specifications, including density, heat of 

29 

combustion,  and  aromatic  content.  Previous  studies  have  shown  that  RP-1  is  a  complex  blend  of 

30 

various hydrocarbons, including paraffins, olefins, and aromatics [4, 5] as shown in Figure 1.2. Due 

31 

to  the  complexity  of  the  composition  of  this  fuel,  it  is  necessary  to  use  less  complex  component 

32 

surrogate model with similar properties for simulation of ignition, flame propagation, heat release, 

33 

and emissions. However, there is a lack of kinetic mechanisms specifically developed for rocket fuels. 

34 

In this regard, other kerosene-based fuels（such as Jet-A and diesel）are often used for comparison 

35 

in combustion studies. Experimental measurements indicate Jet-A that show similar structural classes 

36 

and ignition delay times (IDT) and flame speeds (FS) [6, 7]. Therefore, kerosene and diesel fuel have 

37 

great potential in modeling studies of RP-1.

1.2  Literature review 

41 

1.2.1  CFD study of liquid rocket engine 

42 

Early  combustion simulations of liquid rocket  engines were constrained by limited computational 

43 

resources and therefore relied primarily on global reaction mechanism. The computational studies of 

44 

liquid  rocket  engines  are  summarized  in  Table  1.1.  In  1997,  Beard  et  al.  [8]  applied  equilibrium 

45 

chemistry to evaluate film-cooling performance and showed that RP-1 film injections reduce wall 

46 

temperature with lower specific impulse loss. In 2001, Wang et al. [9] developed a C12H24 surrogate 

47 

fuel step and a quasi-global mechanism, validating its ability to predict combustion efficiency and 

48 

plume radiation. In 2007, Buyn et al. [10] utilized a one-step global reaction coupled with a two-step 

49 

soot formation–oxidation scheme to predict non-gray thermal radiation and soot formation. It was 

50 

found that higher wall emissivity leads to increased radiative heat flux. In 2012, Zhang et al. [11] use 

51 

a one-step global reaction to investigate high-frequency combustion instabilities, showing that the 

52 

abrupt pressure peaks occur near the injector panel.  In 2012, Yang et al. [12] use a 10-species and 

53 

17-reactions mechanism in eddy-dissipation-concept model to evaluate film-cooling, showing that 

54 

higher coolant flow rates and angled film holes improve cooling effectiveness. Furthermore, doping 

55 

RP-1 with a small amount of H2 or H2O was shown to enhance specific impulse loss.  

56 

In 2014, Kim et al. [13] investigated a regeneratively cooled thrust chamber and showed that 

57 

detailed  kerosene  mechanism  containing  209-species  and  1673-reactions  significantly  improve 

58 

predictions  of  combustion  efficiency  and  thrust  coefficient,  whereas  global  mechanism  fails  to 

59 

capture intermediate chemistry and leads to unrealistically high flame temperatures. In 2018, Wang 

60 

et  al.  [14]  conducted  large  eddy  simulation  of  bi-swirl  injectors  under  supercritical  conditions, 

61 

integrating  a  skeletal  mechanism  containing  106  species  and  382  reactions  [15]  with  flamelet 

62 

combustion  model.  The  results  revealed  flame  stabilization  by  recirculation  zones  and  strong 

63 

geometric effects on mixing, thermal protection and oscillatory dynamics. In 2019, Taghavi et al. [16] 

64 

simulated reactive flow in the RD‑108 thrust chamber, validating equivalence ratio effects on engine 

65 

performance and demonstrating that finite‑rate chemistry has good agreement with experimental data. 

66 

In  2021,  Liu  et  al.  [17]  experimentally  and  numerically  investigated  ignition  in  a  rocket-based 

67 

combined  engine  with  a  coaxial  swirl  injector,  showing  that  oxygen  pre-filling  strongly  affects 

68 

ignition  pressure  and  that  flame  stabilization  from  central  recirculation  zones.  Their  simulations 

69 

reproduced  flame-kernel  formation  and  propagation.  In  2024,  Li  et  al.  [18]  performed  3-D  trans-

3 

 
70 

critical large eddy simulation of a swirl coaxial injector using an eddy-dissipation-concept model and 

71 

found  that  combustion  instability  is  driven  primarily  by  pressure  with  velocity  rather  than  swirl-

72 

induced tangential components. These high-fidelity studies highlight the need for accurate real-fluid 

73 

thermodynamics and reliable kinetics mechanisms for combustion modeling.

1.2.2  Chemical kinetic mechanism of RP-1 

81 

RP-1, a kerosene-based rocket propellant, is often modeled using surrogate mixtures composed of 

82 

representative hydrocarbons. From 1995 to 1997, Farmer et al. [3, 4] studied RP-1 and proposed two 

83 

multi-component surrogate mixtures targeting specific compound classes. In 2013, MacDonald et al. 

84 

[19] measured ethylene yields and overall fuel decomposition rates during RP-1 pyrolysis using laser 

85 

absorption in shock tube and developed three-components detailed mechanism consist of n-dodecane, 

86 

methylcyclohexane, iso-cetane (32/59/9 mol%) to validate experimental measurement. 

87 

 Although  few  surrogates  have  been  developed  specifically  for  RP-1  fuels,  extensive  kinetics 

88 

mechanism  research  for  kerosene  and  diesel  fuels  has  been  published.  Table  1.2  summarizes  the 

89 

mechanism of kerosene and diesel fuels. Dagaut et al. [20] proposed four surrogate models containing 

90 

one  to  three  components. Among  them,  the  three-component  surrogate  composed  of  n-decane,  n-

91 

propylbenzene,  n-propylcyclohexane  (74/15/11  mol%)  was  identified  as  the  most  appropriate  for 

92 

predicted  the  jet-stirred-reactor  experimental  results. A  two-component  mixture  of n-decane  and 

93 

1,2,4-trimethylbenzene (80/20wt%), referred to as the Aachen surrogate, was proposed by Honnet et 

94 

al. [21] and was shown to predict the critical conditions of autoignition and soot volume fractions 

95 

observed in kerosene flames. Pei et al. [22] developed a two-component surrogate mechanism for 

96 

diesel fuel, consisting of n-dodecane and m-xylene (77/23 mol%) which was able to reproduce shock 

97 

tube  and  flow  reactor  data.  Narayanaswamy  [23]  developed  a  jet-fuel  surrogate  mechanism 

98 

consisting of n-dodecane, methylcyclohexane, and m-xylene (30.3/21.2/48.5 mol%) which predicted 

99 

ignition  delay  times  in  shock  tubes  across  low  to  high  temperature  range.  Furthermore,  the 

100 

mechanism also reproduces species profiles in flow reactors at high temperatures as well as laminar 

101 

flame speed. 

102 

Table 1.3 summarizes experimental studies on RP-1 oxidation. Recent experimental studies show 

103 

that kerosene, diesel fuel and RP-1 measurement of combustion characteristics such as ignition delay 

104 

times and laminar flame speeds are closely matched, due to its similar component which supports the 

105 

use of kerosene and diesel fuel surrogates to approximate RP-1 combustion behavior.

1.2.3  Reduced chemical kinetic mechanism of RP-1 

117 

Although  RP-1  existing  kinetic  mechanisms  include  only  a  limited  number  of  representative 

118 

components, they remain too large for multidimensional simulations and thus must be reduced for 

119 

computational efficiency. These reduced mechanisms are listed in Table 1.4.   

120 

Previously, several efforts attempted to develop global and quasi-global kerosene mechanisms to 

121 

simplify the modeling of combustion chemistry. In 1981, Westbrook and Dryer [24] proposed one-

122 

step  global  mechanism  capable  of  reproducing  flame  speeds  for  the  hydrocarbon-air  mixtures. 

123 

However, the multiple step quasi-global mechanisms typically consist of two parts: a global kerosene 

124 

step pyrolysis that forms small hydrocarbons, and a global oxidation step for small hydrocarbons. In 

125 

2001, Wang et al. [9] develop a quasi-global mechanism combining C12H24 global steps and wet-CO 

126 

detailed mechanism. In 2010, Franzelli et al. [25] introduced a two-step mechanism  incorporating 

127 

kerosene oxidation and CO-CO2 equilibrium, which predicted the ignition delay time, laminar flame 

128 

speeds and burnt-gas temperature. In 2011, Choi [26] developed a 10-step quasi-global mechanism 

129 

consisting of a kerosene pyrolysis reaction and an ethylene quasi-global mechanism that reproduced 

130 

the ignition delay times. In 2023, Nada et al. [27] proposed a four -step mechanism using C10H20 as 

131 

single  surrogate  fuel,  which  was  able  to  predict  species  profiles  in  perfectly  stirred  reactors  and 

132 

temperature distributions. 

133 

Other  studies  have  developed  reduced  mechanisms  directly  from  large  detailed  kinetic 

134 

mechanisms. In 2004, Luche et al. [28] reduced a detailed mechanism containing 225 species and 

135 

3493  irreversible  reactions  to  a  134  species  and  1220  reactions  mechanism,  which  was  able  to 

136 

reproduce species profiles in perfectly stirred reactors. In 2012, Wang et al. [15] proposed a skeletal 

137 

mechanism consisting of 106 species and 382 reactions, derived from a detailed mechanism with 209 

138 

species and 1673 reversible reactions [20]. This reduced mechanism preserved the accuracy of the 

139 

high-temperature  oxidation  of  kerosene  surrogates,  such  as  ignition  delay  times,  laminar  flame 

140 

speeds and species profiles. In 2018, Koniavitis et al. [29] developed two reduced mechanisms with 

141 

17 and 42 species, both derived from detailed mechanisms containing 367 species and 1892 reactions 

142 

[23]. And these mechanisms were able to reproduce species profiles, ignition strain rates and laminar 

143 

flame speeds.

1.3  Motivation  

149 

RP-1 combustion chemistry plays a central role in the numerical analysis of liquid rocket propulsion. 

150 

However,  RP-1  is  a  highly  complex  multi-component  hydrocarbon  fuel,  and  even  detailed 

151 

mechanisms developed for representative surrogate species remain prohibitively large for practical 

152 

CFD applications. As a result, many previous studies have relied on global mechanisms consisting 

153 

of  only  one  or  two  overall  reaction  steps. Although  such  models  provide  reasonable  flame-shape 

154 

predictions,  their  excessive  simplification  prevents  them  from  capturing  intermediate-species 

155 

formation  and  consumption,  leading  to  severe  overprediction  of  flame  temperature  and  degraded 

156 

accuracy in engine-performance calculations. 

157 

Furthermore,  the  historical  lack  of  RP-1-specific  experimental  data  has  forced  researchers  to 

158 

depend on chemically similar kerosene fuels as substitutes. Recently, two key experimental datasets 

159 

reporting ignition delay times and laminar flame speeds of RP-1 [6, 7]. These measurements now 

160 

provide  the  essential  foundation  for  developing  and  validating  an  intermediate-size  skeletal 

161 

mechanism that balances computational efficiency with chemical fidelity, enabling more predictive 

162 

simulations of RP-1 combustion and ignition. 

163 

164 

1.4  Objective 

165 

The objective of this study is to develop a skeletal chemical kinetic mechanism for an RP-1 surrogate 

166 

to  enable  accurate  and  computationally  efficient  simulations  of  RP-1  combustion  in  liquid  rocket 

167 

engines.  The  surrogate  formulation  consists  of  n-dodecane  (C₁₂H₂₆),  m-xylene  (C₈H₁₀),  and 

168 

methylcyclohexane  (C₇H₁₄)  [23],  adopted  from  multi-component  jet-fuel  surrogate  strategies. The 

169 

reduced  mechanism  is  optimized  for  computational  performance,  allowing  its  application  to 

170 

Reynolds-Averaged  Navier–Stokes  (RANS)  and  Large  Eddy  Simulation  (LES)  frameworks. 

171 

Mechanism reduction is performed using the Parallel-Preconditioning Sensitivity Analysis (PPSA) 

172 

method to remove kinetically insignificant species and reactions while preserving key combustion 

173 

properties such as ignition delay and laminar flame speed. Finally, the skeletal RP-1 mechanism is 

174 

evaluated through three-dimensional reactive-flow simulations of a rocket-engine configuration and 

175 

validated against available experimental measurements.

2.1.1  Mechanism reduction flowchart of RP-1 

180 

The workflow for developing the skeletal mechanism of RP-1 is shown in Figure 2.1. The detailed 

181 

mechanisms of Dagaut et al. (209 species and 1673 reactions) [20], Honnet et al. (118 species and 

182 

527 reactions) [21], Pei et al. (163 species and 887 reactions) [22] and Narayanaswamy et al. (369 

183 

species and 2691 reactions) [23] are evaluated against the RP-1 experimental datasets, the ignition 

184 

delay time of Alturaifi et al. [6], and flame speed of Keesee et al. [7]. From the comparison of IDT 

185 

(see  Figure  2.2),  the  Dagaut  et  al.  [20]  mechanism  overpredicts  ignition  by  one  to  two  orders 

186 

magnitude and matches experimental data only up to an equivalence ratio of 1. The Honnet et al. [21] 

187 

mechanism matches the experimental data well at high-pressure conditions but underpredicts at the 

188 

low-pressure conditions. The Pei et al. mechanism reproduces the experimental data accurately under 

189 

high-temperature  conditions. The  Narayanaswamy  et  al.  [23]    mechanism  shows  good  agreement 

190 

across most of the temperature range.  

191 

In  the  flame  speeds  comparison  (see  Figure  2.3),  all  these  mechanisms  exhibit  a  consistent 

192 

leftward shift in the peak flame speed relative to the experimental data. This mismatch is attributed 

193 

to differences in the average molecular weight between the surrogate fuels used in these mechanisms 

194 

and actual RP-1 sample tested by Keesee  et al. [7]. The  equivalence ratio is determined from  the 

195 

stoichiometric  fuel–air  ratio  based  on  the  average  molecular  weight  of  the  fuel;  consequently, 

196 

surrogates with lighter or heavier effective molecular weights shift the calculated stoichiometric point 

197 

and  lead  to  discrepancies  in  predicted  versus  experimental  flame  speeds.  The  Dagaut  et  al.  [20] 

198 

mechanism underpredicts the peak flame speed, while the Pei et al. [22] mechanism decreases too 

199 

rapidly under rich mixtures. Furthermore, the Honnet et al. [21] mechanism, when applied with its 

200 

two-component surrogate, fails to reproduce flame speeds. 

201 

Overall,  when  evaluated  against  both  ignition  delay  and  flame  speed  measurements,  the 

202 

Narayanaswamy et al. [23] mechanism shows the most accurate ignition delay predictions across the 

203 

full temperature and pressure range. Although its flame speed prediction exhibits a slight peak shift, 

204 

the overall trends and magnitude are closer than other mechanisms. Given its larger number of species 

205 

and  more  detailed  reaction  pathways,  the  mechanism  also  offers  greater  flexibility  for  selective 

206 

reduction while retaining the essential chemical fidelity for RP-1 combustion modeling.

3.1.1  Mechanism reduction using parallel-preconditioning sensitivity analysis (PPSA) 

387 

The PPSA reduction tool is applied to the three-component surrogate mechanism of RP-1 as shown 

388 

in Figure 3.1. As the relative-error tolerance increases in increments of 0.05, the numbers of species 

389 

and reactions decrease. When the tolerance reaches 0.20–0.25, clear deviations appear in both the 

390 

ignition  delay  time  and  flame  speed  predictions.  For  this  reason,  the  reduction  does  not  proceed 

391 

beyond this range. Within this interval, several reduced mechanisms converge to the same size. The

393 

data. However, due to the unavailability of species profile data from jet-stirred reactor experiments 

394
